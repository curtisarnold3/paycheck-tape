The Paycheck Tape

How the money system ate the American income, 1913–2026

  • 2.9¢what a 1913 dollar buys today
  • 2.5 → 5.1 yrsof construction pay to buy a median new home, 1973 vs. 2026
  • 38% → 66%of bank lending going to real estate instead of business, 1947 vs. 2026
  • 3.9% of GDPfederal interest, on its way to 4.6%

Start with a man on a roof. It's 1973, somewhere in the American middle. He frames houses for a living, and at the end of the week he takes home about $5.86 an hour. He has a wife who doesn't work outside the house, two kids, a pickup, and a mortgage on a new three-bedroom that cost $30,200. If you'd asked him whether that was a good deal, he'd have looked at you oddly. It was just what a house cost. Two and a half years of his pay, roughly, and the monthly payment was about a sixth of what he earned.

Now put his grandson on the same roof. Same trade, same union card, same kind of week. He makes $39.36 an hour, which sounds like a fortune to the man from 1973. The median new house he's looking at is $410,700. That's five years of his pay, not two and a half. The payment, at today's rates, is closer to a third of his gross income than a sixth. He's thirty-four and he's renting.

Nothing in that comparison is adjusted for inflation. That's the point. We're not going to argue about what a dollar is worth, because every argument about what a dollar is worth ends in a fight about which index to trust. We're going to count hours. A house is a house, an hour is an hour, and the arithmetic comes straight from the Census Bureau and the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The house costs twice as many working years as it did when the grandfather bought his.

The rest of this tape is an attempt to answer one question: why? Not "who did this to us," because that question has a hundred satisfying and mostly wrong answers. Why, as in: what changed in the machinery, and what does the machinery reward? The answer we'll build, one chart at a time, is that the way money comes into existence, and where it goes once it exists, has been quietly tilting the field for about a century, and steeply since 1980. Everything else, from the price of your insurance to the fact that your dentist works for a private-equity fund, sits downstream of that tilt.

You might not end up agreeing. Every chapter carries a skeptic's rebuttal, because the skeptics are often right about the details. But by the end you'll never look at a mortgage rate, a CPI release, or a bank merger the same way again.

Part I · The machine · Chapter 1

1913

The Federal Reserve, the income tax and the Sixteenth Amendment all arrive in the same year.

  • 9.8 → 335CPI-U, 1913 to Aug 2026
  • 2.9¢what a 1913 dollar buys today
  • −97%purchasing power, by the government's own index

What the chart says

  • A central bank that lends to the government at interest needs a taxpayer to service the interest. The pairing is incentives lining up, not a plan.
  • Interest on Treasuries the Fed itself holds is mostly remitted back. The interest that leaves goes to private and foreign holders.

What a skeptic would say

Progressive-era politics explain the timing, and nominal incomes rose far more than prices. That is exactly why this tape counts hours of work, not dollars.

The chapter

In December 1913, a few days before Christmas, Woodrow Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act. Earlier that same year, in February, the states had finished ratifying the Sixteenth Amendment, which let Congress tax incomes. In October, Congress used the new power and passed the first permanent income tax. Three foundational pieces of the modern American state, all in the span of ten months.

The temptation is to see a plan. Resist it. The people who wanted a central bank had lived through the Panic of 1907, when J.P. Morgan personally had to lock the country's bankers in his library until they agreed to stop the run. The people who wanted an income tax were mostly reformers who thought tariffs fell too hard on farmers. These were different coalitions with different grievances, and the year they happened to converge was 1913.

But notice what fits together once both exist. A central bank lends to the government, and the government pays interest. Interest has to come from somewhere, and a tariff on imported wool is a thin, wobbly source. A tax on every working person's income is a wide, deep one. Nobody had to design that pairing. It just turned out to be extremely convenient, and convenient things get used.

Here's what the pairing has produced over 113 years. In 1913 the government's own price index stood at 9.8. In August 2026 it stood at about 335. Turn that around and a dollar from the year the Fed was born buys, today, a little under three cents' worth of the same stuff. Ninety-seven percent of it is gone. The chart shows the losses arriving in steps rather than a smooth slide, and the steps have dates: 1933, when private gold was called in; 1971, when the last link to gold was cut; 2008, when the Fed started buying assets by the trillion.

A fair objection, and we'll hear it a lot: so what? Wages in 1913 were a few dollars a day. Nominal incomes have risen far more than 34-fold. Losing 97% of a dollar's value means nothing on its own. That's true, and it's why we're not going to spend this tape counting dollars. We'll count hours of work instead. But it's worth knowing the size of the thing before we start measuring around it.

One more piece of honesty. When the Fed holds Treasury bonds, the interest it earns mostly goes back to the Treasury at the end of the year. So the picture of a central bank quietly draining the taxpayer is too simple. The interest that leaves for good is the interest paid to everyone else who holds the debt: pension funds, insurers, foreign governments, you and your 401(k). It's still real money, about a trillion dollars this year. It just has more recipients than the villain story allows.

Sources: FRED CPIAUCNS · Federal Reserve Act (FRASER) · CBO net interest

Part I · The machine · Chapter 2

Money from nothing

Banks create the money they lend. The interest, they don't.

  • $119Ttotal debt outstanding, all sectors
  • $23TM2 money supply
  • 5.1×more owed than exists

What the chart says

  • Bank of England, 2014: when a bank makes a loan it creates a matching deposit — new money. Banks are not intermediaries passing on savings.
  • Every credit-money system so far has needed rising total debt to keep nominal growth going, and the structure rewards that outcome.

What a skeptic would say

Money is a stock; interest is paid from flows, and one dollar can service many debts in a year. Steve Keen has modeled interest being serviced without endless monetary growth. So: an observed pattern and an incentive, not a law of arithmetic.

The chapter

In 2014, three economists at the Bank of England published a short paper with a dull title and a startling claim. Money, they wrote, isn't something banks collect from savers and pass along to borrowers. When a bank makes a loan, it types a number into the borrower's account. That number is new money. It didn't come from anywhere. The textbook story about banks "multiplying up" the cash the central bank gives them, they said politely, was a misconception. The Bank of England said this. Not a blogger. The Bank of England.

A few months later a German economist named Richard Werner did something nobody had bothered to do: he tested it. He borrowed €200,000 from a small cooperative bank and watched its books while the loan was made. No money left anyone else's account. The bank's balance sheet simply got bigger by €200,000 on both sides. One loan, one bank, one afternoon, and the folk theory of banking was dead on the table.

Now hold that thought and look at the chart. The red line is everything owed in the United States by everyone, households, companies, governments: about $119 trillion. The blue line is M2, the broad count of money that actually exists in accounts and wallets: about $23 trillion. Five dollars are owed for every dollar that exists. The shaded gap between the lines is the part that can't be paid back with money, only rolled over.

Here's the mechanism. When the bank creates your mortgage, it creates the principal. It does not create the interest. The interest has to be found somewhere in the rest of the economy, which means somebody else has to borrow, which creates more principal and more unfound interest. Zoom out and you get a system that has to keep growing its debt just to service the debt it already has. Stand still and it seizes.

This is where the book has to be careful, because there's a bad version of this argument and it's everywhere. The bad version says repayment is mathematically impossible, like a proof. It isn't. Money is a stock and interest is paid out of flows; the same dollar can pay interest on a dozen loans in a year as it circulates. Steve Keen, who is no friend of mainstream banking theory, built a model where interest gets serviced without endless monetary growth. And Werner's experiment, as clean as it was, was one loan at one bank.

So we'll say the defensible thing instead: every credit-money system anyone has run has needed ever-rising total debt to keep growing, and the structure rewards exactly that outcome. Not a law. A pattern, and an incentive. That's less satisfying than a proof and much harder to knock down.

Sources: Bank of England, Money creation in the modern economy · Werner 2014, IRFA · FRED TCMDO · FRED M2SL

Part I · The machine · Chapter 3

Where the credit goes

After 1980, bank lending swung from building businesses to bidding on buildings.

  • 38% → 66%real estate share of bank lending, 1947 vs. 2026
  • 76%the 2010 peak
  • 17,885 → 4,238FDIC-insured banks, 1984 vs. 2025
  • 86new bank charters in 15 years, 2010–2025

What the chart says

  • Werner's rule: credit for production grows the economy; credit for consumption raises prices; credit for assets inflates bubbles.
  • Across 17 rich economies, mortgages went from about a quarter of bank lending in 1928 to over 60% by 2007.

What a skeptic would say

Consolidation also has regulatory and technology causes, and the 1920s system of thousands of tiny banks produced 9,000 failures. The lending series includes commercial real estate.

The chapter

Werner, the man with the €200,000 loan, has a second idea that matters more than his first. It's this: it isn't the amount of credit that decides what happens to an economy. It's where the credit goes.

Lend to a factory and you get more stuff; output rises and prices don't. Lend to consumers and you get the same stuff chased by more money; prices rise. Lend to people buying things that already exist, land, buildings, shares, and you get neither more stuff nor more consumption. You get the price of the existing things going up, which looks like wealth right until it doesn't. Bubbles, then banking crises. He calls it the quantity theory of credit, and mainstream economics has mostly ignored it, which doesn't make it wrong.

The first chart is the American version of that idea, drawn from the Fed's own weekly bank survey. It shows real-estate loans as a share of real-estate plus business loans at every commercial bank in the country. In 1947 it was 38%: banks mostly lent to businesses. It drifted up slowly through the postwar decades and crossed 47% around 1980. Then it took off. By 2010 three-quarters of that lending was real estate. Today it's 66%, and it's never come close to going back.

Something flipped around 1980. The banks stopped being mainly in the business of funding businesses and became mainly in the business of funding bids on property. If you're looking for the moment the postwar deal ended, this line is a good candidate, better than any election.

The second chart tells you who was doing the lending. In 1984 there were 17,885 FDIC-insured banks in the United States. There are 4,238 now. The country lost three-quarters of its banks in forty years, and most of them were the small local ones that knew the owner of the machine shop and could lend him money without a credit score. The FDIC itself says that since 2010, fifteen years, only 86 new banks have been chartered. In 1984 alone there were 412.

Put the two charts together. A banking system of thousands of small lenders tied to local businesses turned into a system of a few thousand, dominated by a few dozen, that do big deals. Big deals are real-estate deals, securities deals, buyouts. A giant bank isn't going to underwrite the machine shop. It'll underwrite the fund that buys the machine shop's building.

The critics have real points here. Consolidation was pushed along by branching deregulation in 1994, by the cost of compliance, by software. And nostalgia for the tiny-bank era should remember that 9,000 of those banks failed between 1930 and 1933. The lending series also lumps commercial real estate with mortgages; if a reviewer objects, the residential-only version tells the same story a little more quietly. None of that changes the shape of the line. After 1980, American credit went into buildings.

Sources: FRED REALLN / BUSLOANS · FDIC on new charters · Jordà, Schularick, Taylor, The Great Mortgaging

Part I · The machine · Chapter 4

Grow or die

When money loses value, standing still is shrinking. The interest bill is the receipt.

  • 123%federal debt held by public, % of GDP
  • 3.9% → 4.6%federal interest as % of GDP, now vs. CBO 2036
  • $1.0Tnet interest in FY2026 — more than defense ($885B)

What the chart says

  • Governments, companies and households all have to expand nominal balance sheets just to keep pace with a shrinking unit.
  • CBO: interest goes from about 14% of federal outlays to about 19% over the decade.

What a skeptic would say

Interest hit a similar share of GDP around 1991, so today is not unprecedented. And household debt to GDP peaked in 2008 and has fallen since — the chart shows it.

The chapter

Imagine you're standing on a moving walkway that runs backward. To stay in the same place you have to walk. To get anywhere you have to run. That's what a slowly shrinking unit of money does to everyone who uses it. A company that earns the same dollars this year as last has gotten smaller. A household with the same savings is poorer. A government with the same tax take can buy less. Standing still is shrinking, so everybody runs.

Running, in a credit-money system, means borrowing. The first chart stacks it up: federal debt held by the public, now 123% of GDP, past the World War II record; corporate debt; and household debt, which is the one line that actually fell after 2008. We'll come back to that one, because a book that only shows the lines going up isn't a book anyone should trust.

The second chart is the receipt. It shows what the federal government pays in interest each year as a share of everything the country produces. For fifty years it averaged about 2.1%. It's 3.9% now. The Congressional Budget Office thinks it'll be 4.6% by 2036. In dollars that's about a trillion this year, which is more than the Pentagon gets and more than Medicaid. By the middle of the next decade it's over two trillion, and roughly a fifth of everything Washington spends will be going to people who lent it money.

What's striking isn't the size so much as the direction of travel and how little anyone chose it. Nobody voted to make interest the second-largest federal program. It happened because debt is the way you keep pace on the walkway, and the walkway never stops.

Now the honest caveats. Interest hit about 3.2% of GDP around 1991 and the republic survived, so today's level isn't unprecedented. Companies grow and borrow for competitive reasons under any monetary system; the railroads of the 1880s were up to their necks in debt on a gold standard. And that household line: Americans really did deleverage after 2008, from about 100% of GDP to under 70%. It's the one place on this tape where the arrow points the other way, and it's worth asking why. Part of the answer is that they were forced to. Part is that the borrowing moved somewhere else, to the government, which is what the top line shows.

Sources: CBO, Budget and Economic Outlook Feb 2026 · FRED GFDEGDQ188S · FRED A091RC1Q027SBEA

Part II · The arc · Chapter 5

1913–1945: the baseline

Before the compounding started. The nominal wage quintuples; real pay only doubles.

  • ×5nominal factory wage, 1913–1945
  • ×2the same wage in 2026 dollars
  • 1940first national home-price data — there is none before

What the chart says

  • Treat 1913 as the starting gun, not a data point. This is the thinnest chapter in the book by design.

What a skeptic would say

The period contains WWI inflation, the 1920–21 deflation, 25% unemployment and wartime price controls. The 1913–1919 wage values are still being verified against Historical Statistics of the United States.

The chapter

In 1913 a factory hand in the United States earned about 22 cents an hour. By 1945 it was over a dollar. Five times the pay in thirty-two years sounds like a golden age, and if you'd only looked at the paycheck you'd have thought so.

The chart tells a quieter story. Put the two lines on the same page, what the wage was and what it could buy in today's money, and the second line rises about half as fast as the first. Real pay roughly doubled across the period. That's still real progress, and we shouldn't sneer at it. But it means that even in the very first decades of the Fed's existence, before any of the big monetary decisions of 1933 or 1971, half of the raise was already an illusion. The inflation was there from the start. It was just hidden inside the growth.

We have to be humble about this period, and the chart says so on its face. The numbers come from three different sources stitched together: an old government historical series for the 1910s, a research database for the twenties and thirties, and the modern BLS series from 1939 on. The stitch is marked. The earliest values are still being checked against the original tables. And there's no national home-price series at all before the 1940 Census, so the one comparison we most want to make, what a house cost in hours of work in 1913, can't be made from data. Proxies exist; Robert Shiller has reconstructed a series back to 1890, but it splices sources that don't really belong together.

The period also refuses to sit still. It contains a world war, a brutal deflation in 1920–21, the Crash, a quarter of the workforce unemployed in 1933, and wartime price controls. Any one of those would scramble a thirty-year comparison. That's why we call 1913 the starting gun and not a data point.

What the chapter can honestly say is small but useful. Between the Fed's creation and the end of the war, the nominal wage quintupled, the real wage doubled, and the difference between those two numbers is the thing this book is about. The story gets much clearer, and much better documented, after 1945. That's next.

Sources: NBER Macrohistory M08142 · BLS CES3000000008 · MeasuringWorth

Part II · The arc · Chapter 6

1945–1971: the factory decades

One paycheck bought the house. That wasn't nostalgia. It was arithmetic.

  • 38.8% → 7.9%manufacturing share of jobs, 1943 peak vs. today
  • 1.7 yrsconstruction pay per median home, 1970
  • 69.2%homeownership peak, 2004; 65.0% now

What the chart says

  • From 1948 to the late 1970s, pay rose with productivity. Then the two lines separated and never rejoined.
  • Construction pay per median home (Census values): 2.5 years in 1950, 2.2 in 1960, 1.7 in 1970.

What a skeptic would say

America's rivals were rubble, FHA lending largely excluded Black households, and women's labor-force participation was already rising — the single-income norm was eroding before 1971.

The chapter

In 1950, a construction worker in the United States earned about $1.42 an hour. The typical American house was worth about $7,354. Do the division and you get two and a half years of pay. By 1970 the wage was $4.70, the house was $17,000, and the number had fallen to 1.7 years. Read that again. Across the two decades everyone now calls the golden age, houses got cheaper in the only unit that matters, the hours you had to work for one.

That's why the picture of one paycheck buying a house, a car and a family isn't nostalgia. It's arithmetic, and the arithmetic was improving every year.

The first chart is the engine underneath it. Nearly four in ten American workers made things in a factory at the 1943 peak; through the fifties and sixties it was still roughly one in three. The United States was, for a while, the world's workshop, and the workshop paid well because it was competing for hands. Pay and productivity rose together from 1948 until the late 1970s, a fact the Economic Policy Institute has documented and nobody seriously disputes. Then the two lines separated and never rejoined. Today about 8% of jobs are in manufacturing.

The second chart is what the engine bought. Homeownership climbed from 44% of households in 1940 to 55% in 1950 to 62% in 1960, and kept rising to a peak of 69% in 2004 before sliding back to 65% today. The postwar house wasn't a dream. It was a purchase, made by ordinary people, with money they'd earned that year and the year before.

The skeptics deserve a full hearing here because the golden age carries a lot of freight. America's industrial rivals were rubble in 1945; the dominance was partly the absence of competitors. The FHA mortgages that built the suburbs largely excluded Black families through redlining, so the one-income house was a white house. And women's labor-force participation was already climbing through the sixties; the single-income household was eroding before the monetary story starts. All true, and none of it changes the division problem. For a couple of decades, one working-class wage bought more house each year than it had the year before. Then it stopped.

Sources: EPI productivity–pay gap · Census historical home values · FRED MANEMP / PAYEMS

Part II · The arc · Chapter 7

1971–1980: the gold window closes

August 15, 1971: the anchor comes off. Construction pay peaks two years later and never gets back.

  • 14.8%CPI inflation, March 1980
  • 19.1%Fed funds rate, June 1981
  • −14%construction real pay today vs. its 1973 peak

What the chart says

  • Construction workers earned about 45% more than the average worker in the early 1970s; by 1995, about 15% more.

What a skeptic would say

The Chicago Fed and EPI both credit falling union density, right-to-work laws and prevailing-wage repeal — non-monetary causes. The book's job is to show how inflation and deregulation interacted, not to pretend the other causes aren't there.

The chapter

On a Sunday night in August 1971, Richard Nixon went on television and, in the middle of a speech about wage and price freezes, said something most viewers didn't register. Foreign governments would no longer be able to exchange their dollars for gold. The dollar had been pegged to gold since the end of the war, and every other currency had been pegged to the dollar. Nixon called it temporary. It's been fifty-five years.

Look at the first chart and you can see why he did it. Inflation had been creeping up since 1965, fed by Vietnam and the Great Society, and the gold peg was a promise the Treasury could no longer keep. Then came the oil shocks of 1973 and 1979, the shaded bands, and the country got something the textbooks said couldn't happen: high inflation and high unemployment at the same time. By March 1980 prices were rising almost 15% a year. To stop it, Paul Volcker pushed the Fed's interest rate to 19% in 1981. The cure felt like the disease for a while, but it worked.

Now look at the second chart, which is the one this book is really about. It shows construction hourly pay in today's dollars, from 1947 to now. It rises steadily for a quarter century and peaks in 1972 and 1973, right at the moment the gold window closes. Then it falls, and it falls for twenty years, and it has never made it back. Today a construction worker earns about 14% less, in real terms, than his counterpart did in 1973. In the early seventies construction paid about 45% more than the average job; by 1995 it was 15% more.

Here's the honest complication. The Chicago Fed looked hard at that decline in 2000, and the Economic Policy Institute has too, and both point somewhere other than money. Union membership in construction collapsed. Right-to-work laws spread. States repealed prevailing-wage rules. These are real, documented, non-monetary causes, and any reader who's been on a job site knows they're real.

So what's the monetary story doing here? It's the water the other causes swam in. Inflation at 10% a year makes every wage contract a losing bet for the worker, because the raise you negotiated in January is gone by December. It made the case for deregulation feel urgent. And it's in the years after 1980, when credit stopped going to the businesses that hire construction workers and started going into the buildings themselves, that the wage never recovered. The union story and the money story aren't rivals. They happened to the same man at the same time.

Sources: Nixon, Aug 15 1971 address · Chicago Fed Letter 155 · FRED CES2000000008

Part II · The arc · Chapter 8

1980–2008: offshoring and the FIRE economy

Credit stopped making things and started buying things that already existed.

  • 19.6M → 12.6Mmanufacturing jobs, peak vs. today
  • 17.2M → 11.4Mthe China-shock window, 1999–2011
  • −2.7%net exports as % of GDP; last surplus era ended around 1971
  • 7% → 32%financial share of corporate profits, 1947 vs. 2002 peak

What the chart says

  • Productivity grew about eight times faster than typical pay after 1979.
  • See the credit chart in chapter 3: the swing from business to real-estate lending is the FIRE story in one line.

What a skeptic would say

Manufacturing output kept rising until 2007 and automation explains much of the job loss. The China-shock literature doesn't invoke money at all; the bridge (dollar reserve status, capital inflows) is contested.

The chapter

In 1999, a container ship left Shenzhen with a cargo that would have been unremarkable a decade earlier and was, that year, the beginning of something. Between 1999 and 2011 the United States lost about six million manufacturing jobs, from 17.2 million to 11.4 million. The economists who studied it most carefully, David Autor and his colleagues, put the number attributable to Chinese import competition at two to two and a half million, with about a million in factories themselves, and they called that a conservative lower bound.

The first chart shows what the country did instead of exporting. Green is the era when America sold more to the world than it bought; it ends around 1971, the year the gold window closed. Red is everything since. We've run a trade deficit for half a century, and it's about 2.7% of GDP right now. The second chart is the factory line: a peak near twenty million jobs, a long plateau, and then the China-shock window shaded in gold, when the floor gave way.

Something else was rising as the factories fell, and it's on the chart you saw in chapter 3. After 1980 bank credit swung from business to real estate. The profit center of the economy moved with it. Greta Krippner, a sociologist at Michigan, showed that finance's share of corporate profits reached three to five times its 1950s level. The number people usually quote, that finance went from 20% to 40% of all profits, isn't quite hers and isn't quite right; the Fed's own data put the peak at 32%, in 2002. It's still a stunning number. A third of every dollar of American profit was being made by moving money rather than making things.

Finance, insurance, real estate. The FIRE economy. Credit stopped funding the making of things and started funding the trading of things that already existed. Productivity kept climbing through all of it; the Economic Policy Institute calculates it grew about eight times faster than the typical worker's pay after 1979. The gains went somewhere. They didn't go to the roof.

A skeptic will say, correctly, that manufacturing output kept rising until 2007 and that automation killed at least as many jobs as China did. They'll say the China-shock literature never mentions money at all. The bridge, that the dollar's role as the world's reserve currency pulls in foreign capital and pushes out factories, comes from Matthew Klein and Michael Pettis and is genuinely contested. And a Chicago Fed paper from 1997 argued that if you fix the inflation measure, real wages in the nineties were 18% above 1972, not 16% below. We'll spend all of chapter 10 on that last argument, because it's the whole fight in one sentence.

Sources: Acemoglu et al. 2016 · Autor, Dorn, Hanson, The China Shock · Krippner 2005

Part II · The arc · Chapter 9

2008–now: the wall

QE re-inflated the assets. Wages didn't come along. The next generation hit a wall at household formation.

  • $9.0TFed balance sheet peak, April 2022
  • +237% vs +126%home prices vs. CPI shelter since 2000
  • 32–40median first-time buyer age, depending on the survey
  • 21%first-time buyer share of sales, a record low

What the chart says

  • Zero rates hid the price problem for a decade. When rates normalized, the sticker price was still there and the cheap credit was gone.

What a skeptic would say

The mainstream housing explanation is supply — zoning and permitting (Glaeser & Gyourko). Cheap credit gets capitalized into prices exactly where supply is constrained; the two multiply. NAR's age-40 figure is an outlier; other surveys put it at 32–36.

The chapter

When Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008, the Federal Reserve's balance sheet was about $900 billion. It's a number almost nobody outside the Fed had ever thought about. By the spring of 2022 it was nine trillion. The first chart shows the two great jumps, the one after Lehman and the one during COVID, and you can see that the Fed has only ever managed to walk part of the way back.

The money went where money goes: into assets. That's the second chart, and it might be the single most important picture on this tape, because it's shared with chapter 10 and does double duty. The red line is home prices, up about 237% since 2000. The blue line is what the Consumer Price Index says shelter has cost over the same period, up about 126%. The gap between them is the part of the housing story the official inflation number cannot see, because since 1983 it has measured what a renter would pay, not what a buyer does.

Here's the trick that hid it for a decade. When rates are near zero, a bigger price doesn't mean a bigger payment. From 2010 to 2020 house prices climbed and mortgage payments, measured in hours of work, stayed almost flat. It felt fine. Then rates went back to normal in 2022 and the sticker price was still there, but the cheap credit that had made it bearable was gone. That's the wall.

The people who hit it are the ones trying to form a household for the first time. The National Association of Realtors reported in late 2025 that first-time buyers made up 21% of purchases, the lowest share it has ever recorded, and that the median first-time buyer was forty years old. We should be careful with that forty. NAR's survey is a mail-in questionnaire with a low response rate, and other sources, the New York Fed, the Mortgage Bankers Association, put the age closer to 32 to 36. So call it 32 to 40, depending on who's counting. Every source agrees on the direction. It was 28 in 1992.

The mainstream housing story is about supply: zoning, permits, the cost of building, and it's a real story with serious economists behind it. But cheap credit and tight supply aren't competing explanations. They multiply. When you can't build more houses, every extra dollar the banks are willing to lend goes straight into the price of the ones that exist. Which brings us back to chapter 3. The credit went into buildings, and the buildings got expensive, and the kids can't buy them.

Part III · The measurements · Chapter 10

How the CPI stopped measuring the cost of living

Every methodology change since 1983 lowered the reported number. The largest inflation-indexed debtor on earth writes the methodology.

  • 40 → 62 weeksof median male pay to cover a family's basics, 1985 vs. 2022
  • +106% vs +77%True Living Cost vs. CPI, 2001–2024
  • 0.15–0.2 pts/yrBLS's own estimate of what its changes took off the index

What the chart says

  • 1983: home prices swapped for owners' equivalent rent. 1999: geometric mean, always lower than arithmetic. 2002: chained CPI, ~0.25 points lower again.
  • Social Security, federal pensions, TIPS, tax brackets and the 'real' yield on Treasuries are all keyed to CPI. Every tenth of a point saved is money the Treasury keeps.
  • Two presidents of opposite parties, 54 years apart, punished the statistics bureau for numbers they disliked: Nixon in 1971 (personnel, not data), and the 2025 commissioner firing.

What a skeptic would say

The academic consensus is that CPI overstates a fixed-basket, constant-quality price level, and BLS says hedonics add only ~0.005 points a year net. Conceded. The claim here is different: CPI doesn't measure what it costs to form a household, and it never claimed to. ShadowStats gets the direction right and the arithmetic wrong; its number is not used anywhere on this tape.

The chapter

In January 1971, an assistant commissioner at the Bureau of Labor Statistics named Harold Goldstein did something dangerous. Asked about a small drop in unemployment, he called it "marginally significant," which was accurate and which contradicted the Labor Secretary's cheerier version. That summer the Nixon tapes record the president discussing "action on Goldstein," and, in a passage historians still wince at, asking whether the bureau's senior staff were Jewish. A memo counted them. By September, Goldstein had been moved to a job where he wouldn't talk to reporters, and the bureau's twenty-year tradition of professional press briefings was quietly ended.

Nothing in that story shows a number being altered. Read the record carefully and you find a president controlling the interpretation and the personnel, and succeeding on personnel. That's the point. Fifty-four years later, in August 2025, a president of the opposite party fired the commissioner of the BLS hours after a weak jobs report, calling the numbers rigged without offering evidence. Two presidents, two parties, one lesson for everyone who works in the building: this is what happens to people who deliver bad news.

Now ask who benefits from a low inflation number. Social Security cost-of-living raises are keyed to the CPI. So are federal pensions, the principal on inflation-protected bonds, and the income-tax brackets. The federal government is the largest inflation-indexed debtor on earth, and every tenth of a point shaved off the index is money the Treasury keeps, across all of those programs at once. This wasn't hidden. The Boskin Commission was convened in 1995 by the Senate Finance Committee precisely because of the CPI's role in indexing benefits, and its report lives on the Social Security Administration's website.

Then look at what happened to the methodology. In 1983 the BLS stopped counting home prices and started counting what a homeowner would hypothetically pay to rent their own house. In 1999 it switched to a geometric-mean formula for most of the index, which is always lower than the plain average. In 2002 it introduced a chained CPI that runs another quarter point lower, and in 2017 Congress adopted it for the tax brackets. Every change has a defensible technical rationale. Every change lowered the number. The BLS's own research series, which applies today's methods back to 1978, admits a cumulative gap of about five points on the price level, roughly 0.15 to 0.2 points a year. Compound that for forty years and you get an 8% understatement, and every real-wage series built on the CPI overstates by the same amount.

That's the government's number. The two charts show what independent measurers find. The Cost-of-Thriving Index counts weeks of a median man's pay needed to cover a family's housing, health insurance, car, food and college. In 1985 it was under forty weeks. In 2022 it was sixty-two: more than a year of work to buy what took ten months. The American Enterprise Institute rebutted it, and their corrected version says the increase is closer to ten weeks than twenty-two. Even the rebuttal concedes the direction. The second chart is the Ludwig Institute's True Living Cost, which prices only essentials and finds them up 106% since 2001 against the CPI's 77%. Deflate median earnings by that index and they're down since 2001. Deflate them by the CPI and they're up.

We should be as honest about the dissidents as about the government. John Williams of ShadowStats was early and loud and right about the direction of every one of these changes, and he deserves the credit. But his method takes the BLS's estimate of the cumulative effect and applies it every year, compounding, which yields 10% annual inflation and implies prices have quintupled since 2005. Asked for his calculations in 2008 he said he wasn't going to redo the CPI. A hostile reviewer will put that quote next to any ShadowStats citation, so there are none on this tape. Everything Williams got right is available from better sources.

And the skeptics' strongest point: the academic consensus is that the CPI overstates inflation for a fixed basket of constant-quality goods, and the BLS says hedonic adjustments net out to almost nothing. Fine. Concede it. The book's claim is different. The CPI doesn't measure what it costs to form a household, buy a home, pay for care or finance an education, and it has never claimed to. That's why this tape counts hours of work. There's nothing to hedonically adjust in an hour.

Part III · The measurements · Chapter 11

GDP: what it counts and what it hides

GDP counts the rent you'd pay yourself, the bank's markup and the hospital bill. All as growth.

  • 7% → 32% → 20%financial share of US corporate profits, 1947 / 2002 peak / 2025
  • 22.6%finance, insurance, real estate and leasing as % of GDP
  • ~17–18%health spending as % of GDP

What the chart says

  • Kuznets, who built the number, warned Congress in 1934 that it was not a measure of welfare.
  • When the FIRE economy grows, GDP grows — even as the things GDP was invented to approximate get worse.

What a skeptic would say

Economists already agree GDP isn't a welfare measure, and free digital goods push measured GDP the other way. ShadowStats' GDP series inherits the same arithmetic error as its CPI series and is excluded.

The chapter

In 1934, a Russian-born economist named Simon Kuznets delivered a report to the Senate that invented, more or less, the number we now call GDP. He'd been asked to measure how much the American economy had shrunk in the Depression, and to do that he had to decide what counted as economic activity and what didn't. On page seven of the report, before anyone had a chance to misuse it, he put in a warning: the welfare of a nation, he wrote, can scarcely be inferred from a measure of national income. He was worried people would treat the number as a scoreboard. Ninety years later, it's the only scoreboard anyone reads.

Here's some of what the scoreboard counts. If you own your home, the government estimates what you'd pay yourself in rent and adds it to GDP; that imputed rent is something like 8 or 9% of the whole economy, and it rises when house prices rise. When a bank charges you more for a loan than it pays a depositor, the spread is counted as a service the bank produced. When you go to the hospital, the bill is growth. Health spending is now around 17 to 18% of GDP, and every dollar of it makes the number go up whether or not anyone got healthier.

The first chart is the one that finally settles an argument. People who write about financialization love to say that finance went from 20% of corporate profits to 40%. It comes from a chart in Greta Krippner's 2005 paper, read by other authors, not from her text. So here's the number from the Fed's own flow-of-funds accounts: the financial sector made 7% of American corporate profits in 1947, peaked at 32% in 2002, and takes about 20% today. Thirty-two, not forty. It's still a fivefold rise, and it's sourced.

The second chart is finance, insurance, real estate and leasing as a share of GDP, running around 22 to 23%. It includes that imputed rent, which is exactly the point. When the FIRE economy grows, GDP grows. When house prices double, GDP rises even though nobody built anything. The number Kuznets built to approximate national well-being now rewards the things that make well-being harder to reach.

Economists will tell you, correctly, that they already know GDP isn't a welfare measure; there's a whole literature, including a commission led by two Nobel laureates, saying so. They'll add that free digital goods push measured GDP the other way, undercounting what we get. Both fair. The trouble isn't the economists. It's that every politician, every central banker and every headline treats the number as the score, and the number has a thumb on the scale for asset prices. One more note: ShadowStats publishes an alternative GDP series with the same arithmetic problem as its inflation series. It isn't used here.

The chapter

This is the chapter the rest of the book was built to reach, so let's slow down and do the sum in public.

Take the median price of a new house in the United States each year, from the Census Bureau. Take what a construction worker earned per hour that year, from the BLS. Multiply the wage by 2,080, a full-time year. Divide the house by the answer. That's the chart: years of pay per house. No inflation adjustment, no index, no basket, no hedonic anything. In 1973 the number was 2.5. It climbed through the eighties and nineties to about 4.5 and sat there for a while. Then it went to six during the bubble, fell back to four and a half in the bust, and ran up to 6.6 years in April 2022, the highest ever measured. It's 5.1 now. The house costs twice the working life it did when the grandfather bought his.

Now the payment, which is the number people actually live with. The second chart shows what the monthly principal and interest on that median new house would be as a share of a construction worker's gross pay, assuming a 20% down payment and a thirty-year loan at that year's rate, against the 28% line lenders use as a ceiling. In 1973 it was 17%. In 1980, with rates at 13%, it was 35%, and the eighties and nineties never really got below 30. Then something odd happens. From 2010 to 2020 the price keeps rising and the payment falls to the low twenties, because rates fell to 3%. Cheap credit hid the price problem for a decade. In 2022 rates normalized and the payment went straight back over 30%. The price was still there. The trick was gone.

The table underneath lays out the anchor years, and it's worth a minute with it. In 1973 the payment was $172 a month. Today it's $2,072. Wages went up about seven times over the same span. The payment went up twelve.

The skeptics have two strong objections and they should be answered squarely. First, new houses have roughly doubled in size since the early 1970s, so we're comparing a 1,500-square-foot ranch with a 2,400-square-foot two-story. A per-square-foot version of this chart shrinks the gap. It doesn't erase it, and there's a catch: you can't go buy a 1973-sized new house, because nobody builds them. Second, supply. Zoning and permitting really do restrict building, and where supply is restricted, prices rise. True, but that's a multiplier on the credit story, not a replacement for it. Every extra dollar banks are willing to lend into a market that can't build goes into the price of what's already standing.

One choice we made on purpose: the payment chart uses a single construction wage, not a two-earner household. Most households now have two incomes and could carry more. That's the point. The house that one income used to buy now needs two, and the second earner isn't buying a bigger life. She's buying the same house.

Insurance

Denial is profitable because almost nobody appeals.

  • 19%of in-network ACA marketplace claims denied, 2023
  • 1%–54%range by insurer
  • ~1%of denials appealed
  • ~6%of denials that cited medical necessity

What the chart says

  • Insurance is compulsory — health, auto, mortgage — and everything is insurable. The business model collects premiums and adjudicates claims.
  • Allstate: an Oklahoma attorney-general suit (July 2026) alleges a 'Disaster Payment Minimization Scheme.' UnitedHealth: plaintiffs allege over 90% of AI-driven denials are reversed when appealed. All allegations; nothing ruled.

What a skeptic would say

KFF's own data show in-network denials roughly steady since 2015 — 'persistently high and opaque,' not 'surging after 2020.' No national property-and-casualty denial series exists. Premiums do track repair costs, catastrophes and reinsurance; P&C underwriting lost money in 2022–23.

The chapter

In 2023, insurers selling plans on HealthCare.gov denied about one in five of the claims that came from doctors and hospitals in their own networks. That's the average. Individual companies ranged from 1% to 54%; some insurers say no to more than half of what their own contracted providers bill them. Out of network the figure was 37%. Of all those denials, only around six in a hundred cited medical necessity, the reason most people assume drives a refusal.

Here's the number that explains the business. About 1% of denied claims were appealed. The other 99% were paid by the patient, written off by the doctor, or simply abandoned. If you're an insurer, a denial is nearly free: it costs a form letter, and ninety-nine times out of a hundred it sticks. Denial isn't a medical judgment. It's a pricing strategy that works because almost nobody pushes back.

What makes this an attack on purchasing power rather than a mere annoyance is that you can't opt out. Health coverage is required by law, or by the tax code, or by your employer. Auto insurance is mandatory in nearly every state. A mortgage requires homeowner's insurance. And the industry has learned to insure everything else: your phone, your dog, your vacuum cleaner, your eyesight. The premium is compulsory. The payout is discretionary.

The Allstate matter is the case study, and everything in it is an allegation until a court says otherwise. In July 2026 the Oklahoma attorney general sued the company, alleging what the complaint calls a "Disaster Payment Minimization Scheme": limiting field adjusters, routing claims through outside reviewers, applying standards policyholders never saw. A New Mexico homeowner's suit over a $130,000 hail claim alleges the practices trace back to a McKinsey redesign of the claims process in the 1990s. And in the health world, a class action against UnitedHealth alleges that an algorithm was used to deny post-acute care, that over 90% of those denials were reversed when appealed, and that roughly 0.2% of policyholders ever appealed. A federal judge ordered broad discovery in March 2026. Allegations, all of it. But the discovery orders are real, and so is the pattern they describe.

The book has a weak spot here and you should know it. The denial data comes from KFF, and KFF's own finding is that in-network denials have been roughly steady since 2015, 16% one year, 19% the next. That supports "persistently high and opaque." It does not support "surging after COVID," and there's no national series at all for property and casualty denials, so the hail-damage story rests on lawsuits rather than statistics. Insurers, for their part, will tell you premiums track repair costs, catastrophes and reinsurance, and that the industry lost money on underwriting in 2022 and 2023. That's true too. It just doesn't explain why 99% of denials go unchallenged, or why that's such good business.

Part IV · The attacks · Chapter 14

Healthcare capture

Your doctor doesn't own the practice anymore. The owner's incentives replace the clinician's.

  • 60% → 42%physicians in private practice, 2012 vs. 2024
  • 7% → 16%dentists affiliated with a DSO, 2015 vs. 2024
  • >1,000%PBM markup on 22% of specialty generics (FTC)
  • $88Bmedical debt on credit reports, 2021

What the chart says

  • Hospitals, corporations and private-equity funds bought the practices with cheap post-2008 leverage. Volume, billing and prescribing follow ownership.
  • The three largest PBMs took over $7.3B in dispensing revenue above acquisition cost in 2017–22.

What a skeptic would say

Consolidation is also driven by Medicare site-of-service payment differentials, the 340B program and administrative burden. About 73% of dentists still own their practices. PBM markups reflect vertical integration more than monetary policy.

The chapter

In 2012, six in ten American physicians worked in a practice owned by physicians. In 2024 it was four in ten. Somewhere around 80,000 doctors, in twelve years, stopped working for themselves and started working for a hospital system, a corporation, or a private-equity fund. The American Medical Association's own survey documents it. Dentists are a few years behind on the same path: 7% of them were in a corporate dental chain in 2015, 16% in 2024, and among dentists less than five years out of school, nearly a third.

Why does it matter who signs the doctor's paycheck? Because whoever owns the practice sets the incentives, and the incentives of an owner aren't the incentives of a clinician. An owner wants volume, billing that maximizes reimbursement, prescribing that flows through affiliated pharmacies, and rigorous compliance with whatever the insurers will pay for. A doctor who owns her own practice can decide a patient doesn't need the follow-up. An employed one has a productivity target.

Follow the drug through the system and the incentives get vivid. In January 2025 the Federal Trade Commission published its second report on pharmacy benefit managers, the three companies that stand between drugmakers and your prescription. It found they'd marked up 22% of specialty generic drugs by more than a thousand percent, and taken over seven billion dollars in dispensing revenue above what the drugs cost them between 2017 and 2022. Their own affiliated pharmacies' share of specialty prescriptions rose from 54% to 68%. The middleman owns the counter.

What's left over when the system is done is medical debt. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau counted $88 billion of it on credit reports as of 2021. That's the residue: the part of the bill the insurer denied, the practice's owner billed, and the patient couldn't pay.

Where does money come in? Through the buyouts. The private-equity wave in health care ran on cheap leverage; Gondi and Song, writing in JAMA in 2019, drew the line from post-2008 interest rates to the funds rolling up dermatology, dentistry and emergency rooms. Cheap credit doesn't just inflate houses. It inflates anything with a steady cash flow, and few things have a steadier cash flow than a patient who has to come back.

The counterarguments are real. Medicare pays hospitals more than independent doctors for the same service, which by itself pushes practices into hospital ownership. The 340B drug program and the sheer administrative burden of modern medicine do the same. About 73% of dentists still own their practices, so the dental story is early. And the PBM markups are a story about vertical integration more than monetary policy. Granted. The book's claim is narrower: cheap credit made the roll-up possible, and the roll-up changed who the doctor works for.

Part IV · The attacks · Chapter 15

Property tax by phantom valuation

The cheapest homes are assessed at twice the ratio of the priciest. Asset inflation makes it worse.

  • 1.55× vs 0.72×assessment ratio, cheapest 5% vs. priciest 5% of homes
  • ~2.15×the bottom-to-top gap, 26 million sales
  • ~40%average Colorado property-tax bill increase cited in the 2023 special session

What the chart says

  • Municipalities depend on property tax, so assessed values ratchet up with the market and lag on the way down.
  • In Chicago alone, roughly $2.2B in tax burden shifted from high-value to low-value homes.

What a skeptic would say

Berry's finding is assessment error, not general overvaluation. Colorado's effective rate was 0.55% in 2021, third-lowest in the country; the 2023 spike followed the repeal of the Gallagher amendment.

The chapter

Christopher Berry, a professor at the University of Chicago, did something patient. He took twenty-six million home sales across about 2,600 counties, from 2007 to 2017, and compared each sale price with what the county assessor had said the house was worth for tax purposes. If assessment were fair, the ratio would be about the same for a cheap house and an expensive one. It isn't. The cheapest 5% of homes in a jurisdiction are assessed at 1.55 times the local average ratio. The most expensive 5% are assessed at 0.72 times. Bottom to top, that's a gap of more than two to one. The poorest homeowners in America pay property tax on a value that's systematically too high, and the richest pay on one that's too low.

That's the chart, and the points on it were traced from Berry's own figure because he never published the table. The precision is a couple of hundredths either way. The shape isn't in doubt. Berry checked whether it could be measurement error or deliberate policy and concluded it couldn't. In Chicago alone he estimated about $2.2 billion in tax burden had been shifted from expensive homes to cheap ones.

Now connect it to the rest of the tape. Cities and counties live on property tax; it's the bulk of local tax revenue almost everywhere. When house prices inflate, assessed values follow them up. When prices fall, assessments lag on the way down, and Berry's later work with a coauthor shows that lag makes the regressivity worse. So an asset-price boom driven by credit turns into a tax increase, and the increase lands hardest on the people who benefited least from the boom.

Colorado is the case close to home. In 2023 assessed values reset after the pandemic run-up, and the governor's call for a special session cited an average bill increase of around 40%. Voters rejected the legislature's proposed fix, Proposition HH, 59 to 41. The legislature came back in a special session and cut the residential assessment rate and raised the exemption. It was a real fight over a phantom: nobody's house had produced 40% more of anything. The number on the assessor's letter had simply followed the market up.

Be fair to the other side. Berry's finding is about assessment error, not about houses being overvalued in general. Colorado's effective property-tax rate was 0.55% in 2021, third-lowest in the country, and the 2023 shock was set up by the repeal of the Gallagher amendment in 2020, a policy decision as much as a market one. There's also no national data on what happens when people appeal. What Berry proves is narrower and worse: the machinery that turns asset prices into tax bills is tilted, and it tilts toward the cheap house.

Financial servicers and the death of recourse

You can't sue. You can arbitrate, or wait three years for pennies.

  • $33Bfederal class settlements, 2006–07
  • ~15%of that to attorneys
  • ~9%median share of class members who ever file a claim
  • ~3 yrstypical time to settlement approval

What the chart says

  • Arbitration clauses, class actions that pay out to almost nobody, and regulators that don't act: the individual has no practical remedy against a large financial institution.
  • Regulation E gives you the right to stop a preauthorized transfer with three business days' notice. Try it.

What a skeptic would say

Fitzpatrick himself argues class lawyers are underpaid; the loss to class members is the claims rate, not the fee share. Arbitration defenders cite lower cost and speed. This chapter's link to money is the weakest in the book — it's a consolidation story.

The chapter

Try, sometime, to stop an automatic payment. Not the streaming service. The mortgage servicer, the one that bought your loan from the bank that made it and now takes a payment out of your account each month. Federal regulation is clear: under Regulation E you can revoke a preauthorized transfer with three business days' notice. The book's author tried. What he found is the on-ramp to this chapter: the rule exists, and the servicer doesn't care, and there is no one to call who can make it care.

That's the shape of the problem. An individual has no practical remedy against a large financial institution. The account agreement you clicked through requires arbitration, in a forum the company selected, so you can't sue. If a class action does get filed, here's what it looks like from the inside. Brian Fitzpatrick, a Vanderbilt law professor, studied every federal class settlement in 2006 and 2007: 688 of them, worth about $33 billion. Around $5 billion, 15%, went to the lawyers. The typical case took about three years to reach approval. And then the part almost nobody talks about: the Federal Trade Commission later found that in a typical settlement, about 9% of eligible class members ever file a claim. The other 91% of the money either goes back to the defendant, gets redirected, or sits.

The chart shows where Fitzpatrick's $33 billion went. It's tempting to read it as "the lawyers took it all," and that's not what the data say; Fitzpatrick himself argues class lawyers are underpaid for the risk they carry. The real loss is the claims rate. A remedy that 9% of victims collect isn't a remedy. It's a cost of doing business, priced in advance.

The regulators were supposed to be the third option. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau did a two-year study of arbitration and wrote a rule in 2017 to limit it; Congress overturned the rule within months. In 2025 the bureau's enforcement work was sharply curtailed. The complaint database is still there, and mortgage servicing fills a good share of it. What's missing is anyone obliged to act on it.

This chapter's link to money is the weakest in the book, and we'd rather say so than pretend. Inflation didn't write your arbitration clause. What did is consolidation, chapter 3's story: a banking and servicing industry with a few thousand firms instead of tens of thousands, big enough that no single customer's complaint is worth the cost of answering it. When your lender was in your town, recourse was a conversation. When it's a servicer in another state that holds two million loans, recourse is a form, and the form goes nowhere. Arbitration's defenders will say it's cheaper and faster than court, and for the company it is.

The service collapse

When a job can't buy a life, nobody tries.

  • 3.0%quits rate peak, November 2021
  • $11 → $21leisure & hospitality real hourly pay, 1964 vs. 2026 (2026 $)
  • 62 weeksof median pay to cover a family's basics — more than a year

What the chart says

  • Real pay in bars, restaurants and hotels nearly doubled over 62 years. The cost of thriving rose faster, and a house went from 2.5 years of pay to five.
  • Churn, disengagement, tip screens: third-world service at first-world prices.

What a skeptic would say

Low-wage workers saw the fastest real gains of anyone in 2020–23 — the chart shows it. Customer-satisfaction indexes have been roughly flat for long stretches, and tip prompts are a payment-technology story.

The chapter

There's a particular look you learn to recognize behind a counter. It isn't rudeness. It's absence. The person taking your order is there in body and gone in every other way, and you can't really blame them, because the job is a place to stand until something else happens and nothing else is happening.

The numbers underneath that look are on the two charts. The first is hourly pay in bars, restaurants and hotels, in today's dollars, since 1964. It starts around $11 and ends around $21. That's not flat, and this tape won't pretend it is; real pay in the sector nearly doubled over sixty years, with a striking chunk of the gain coming after 2015, and especially in 2020 through 2023, when the lowest-paid workers in America saw the fastest raises of anyone. Autor, Dube and McGrew documented it. It's the one bright spot in the whole labor story and it belongs on the chart.

Now put it next to what the job has to buy. In 1985 a median male worker needed under forty weeks of pay to cover a family's basics. In 2022 it was sixty-two. A house went from two and a half years of a tradesman's pay to five. Service pay went up, and what it had to reach went up faster, and by a lot. A doubling of real wages doesn't mean much when the entry ticket to an ordinary life tripled.

The second chart is what people do about it. The quits rate, from the government's monthly survey, hit 3% in late 2021, the highest on record, and it's still elevated. People don't quit jobs they can build a life on. They quit jobs that are interchangeable, and a job whose pay can't buy the life is interchangeable by definition. The customer sees the result as churn, as tip screens, as the absent look. Third-world service at first-world prices, and the worker didn't cause it any more than you did.

What connects this to money? Not the wage directly. It's that the things a wage has to buy, above all housing, were repriced by credit, chapters 3 and 12, while the wage was repriced by the labor market. Two different machines, running at two different speeds, and the gap between them is where ambition used to be. Skeptics will point out that customer-satisfaction indexes are roughly flat over long stretches, and that tip prompts are a story about payment tablets, not despair. Fair. The quits line isn't flat, though, and the sixty-two weeks aren't either.

Part IV · The attacks · Chapter 20

Additional attacks on purchasing power

Congress indexed the tax brackets to inflation. It never indexed the thresholds.

  • $600 → $7,4721099 reporting threshold, set 1954, if indexed today
  • $3,000 → $15,413capital-loss cap, set 1978
  • $25,000 → $80,757Social Security benefit taxation, set 1984
  • $250,000 → $522,427home-sale gain exclusion, set 1997

What the chart says

  • Set in statute, plainly inflation-driven, hard to dispute. The strongest candidate for Part IV.
  • Other candidates: shrinkflation, junk fees, subscription creep, student debt, childcare, auto insurance, utilities. Each has a primary source and a caveat.

What a skeptic would say

The CPI already adjusts for package downsizing, so shrinkflation barely moves the index. Net tuition has been flat at many public colleges since about 2012. Enactment years still need statute verification.

The chapter

In 1954, when Congress rewrote the tax code, it decided that anyone paying a contractor more than $600 in a year had to file a form reporting it. Six hundred dollars was serious money then, a couple of months' wages for a lot of people. Congress never changed the number. Today $600 is a weekend. If the threshold had been indexed to inflation the way tax brackets are, it would be about $7,472. It's still $600, which means the reporting net has silently widened by a factor of twelve, catching people it was never written to catch.

That's the pattern in the chart, five times over. The cap on capital losses you can deduct against ordinary income was set at $3,000 in 1978; indexed, it would be over $15,000. Social Security benefits started being taxed in 1984 for single retirees with income over $25,000, a threshold that was meant to touch only the comfortable. Indexed, it would be about $80,000. It's still $25,000, so it now touches most retirees with any savings. The exclusion on the gain from selling your house, $250,000 for a single filer, dates to 1997 and would be over half a million if it had kept pace. The net investment income tax, set at $200,000 in 2013, has already slipped 40% in real terms.

Here's why this belongs in a book about money. Congress indexed the tax brackets in the 1980s, so you don't get pushed into a higher rate just because inflation raised your nominal pay. But it left the thresholds alone, and a frozen threshold in an inflating currency is a tax increase that nobody voted for and nobody has to defend. It happens automatically, every year, in the direction that favors the Treasury. And since 2017 even the brackets are indexed to the chained CPI, which runs a quarter point a year below the regular one, chapter 10 again. Each of these is set in statute, plainly inflation-driven, and almost impossible to argue with. That's why it's the strongest of the candidates on this page.

The others are worth knowing and worth handling carefully. Shrinkflation, the smaller box at the same price, is real, but the BLS already prices by unit, so it barely moves the index. Junk fees got an FTC rule in 2024 that was narrowed to tickets and lodging. The click-to-cancel rule for subscriptions was vacated by a court in 2025. Student debt sits around $1.7 trillion, though net tuition at many public colleges has been flat since about 2012. Childcare and utilities are rising for reasons that are partly about labor costs and grid investment rather than money. Each has a primary source and each has a caveat, and the caveats are on the page so a hostile reader can't say we hid them. The enactment years in the chart still need to be checked against the statutes.

Which brings the tape back to where it started. A man on a roof, his grandson on the same roof, and a house that costs twice the working years it did. Nothing in that comparison needed an inflation adjustment, and nothing in it needed a villain. It needed a machine that creates money as credit, sends the credit into things that already exist, and rewards everyone who plays along. We've drawn the machine one chart at a time. Whether we fix it is the only question left.

Fixes that ignore where credit goes will be undone by where credit goes.

What the chart says

  • Three sourced links form the chain. Money is created as bank credit; credit has shifted to real estate and financial assets; asset inflation feeds housing, property tax, private-equity roll-ups and federal interest.
  • For insurance denials, recourse, PBMs and service quality, consolidation and regulatory design are the proximate causes. Monetary expansion is the background condition that rewards scale, leverage and asset ownership over wages.

What a skeptic would say

Germany, Japan and Switzerland run the same fiat credit-money system with very different housing, healthcare and legal outcomes. So institutions and credit allocation matter — which is exactly where chapter 19 goes.

The chapter

By now you've seen twenty-odd charts and one chain diagram, and the honest question is whether the chain holds or whether we've just lined up things that happen to be bad at the same time.

So let's say only what the sources support. Link one: money is created when banks lend. That's the Bank of England and Werner, chapter 2, and it isn't seriously disputed anymore. Link two: since 1980, the credit banks create has gone mostly into real estate and financial assets rather than business. That's the Fed's own lending data, chapter 3. Link three: when credit pours into things that already exist, their prices outrun wages. That's the home-price line against the shelter index, chapter 9, and the years-of-pay chart, chapter 12. Link four: asset inflation feeds directly into the cost of housing, into property-tax assessments, into the private-equity roll-ups of medicine and dentistry, and into the federal interest bill. Chapters 12, 15, 14 and 4. Link five: a household that has to work twice as many years for the same house forms later, or doesn't form. Chapter 9.

Each link is sourced. That's the argument. Not a hidden hand; a machine with a bias, and the bias is toward the things that already exist and the people who already own them.

Here's the part we'd rather not overclaim. Insurance denials, arbitration clauses, PBM markups and the absent look behind the counter aren't caused by money creation in any direct sense. Their proximate causes are consolidation and regulatory design. What monetary expansion does is set the background conditions: it rewards scale, because scale gets the cheapest credit; it rewards leverage, because inflation erodes the debt; and it rewards owning assets over earning wages, because assets are what the credit inflates. In that environment, consolidation isn't a conspiracy. It's the winning strategy, and everyone eventually plays it.

That's what "none of it can be fixed without fixing money" actually means. Not that a monetary reform would cure insurance denials. That any fix which ignores where credit goes will be undone by where credit goes. Cap the property tax and the assessments will find another route. Break up the hospital chains and cheap leverage will reassemble them. You can treat the symptoms one at a time forever, and the country has been.

The strongest objection points across the Atlantic and the Pacific. Germany, Japan and Switzerland run fiat credit-money systems just like ours, and their housing, healthcare and legal outcomes are very different. Doesn't that break the chain? No, it points at the link that matters. They have the same kind of money. They allocate the credit differently, and they have very different banks. Which is the next chapter.

Part V · The root · Chapter 19

What a fix would look like

Not gold. Thousands of small banks lending to businesses that make things.

  • $1.0T → $2.1Tnet federal interest, FY2026 to FY2036 (CBO)
  • 14,496 → 4,238US banks, 1984 vs. 2025
  • 86new charters in 15 years; 412 in 1984 alone

What the chart says

  • Credit-allocation reform: local banks tied to local business (Germany's Sparkassen; the US before 1984), credit guidance toward production, and structural separation of banking from speculation.
  • Even the FDIC says it needs to find ways to encourage more new bank formation.

What a skeptic would say

No economist on the IGM panel thought a gold standard would improve outcomes. Credit guidance risks cronyism and helped inflate Japan's 1980s bubble. Small banks failed en masse in the 1930s and the S&L crisis. Glass-Steagall wouldn't have covered the 2008 institutions.

The chapter

The first thing to say about the fix is what it isn't. It isn't gold. In 2012 the University of Chicago asked a panel of prominent economists across the political spectrum whether a return to the gold standard would improve price stability or employment. Not one said yes. The gold era had its own panics, in 1873, 1893 and 1907, and the last one is why the Fed exists. Nostalgia for a metal isn't a plan.

The plan, if the chain in chapter 18 is right, has to be about where credit goes and who decides. And there's a country that's been running the experiment for two hundred years. Germany's Sparkassen and cooperative banks are small, local, legally tied to the towns they serve, and they lend overwhelmingly to the businesses in those towns. Research in the Journal of Banking and Finance found they lend less cyclically than commercial banks, meaning they don't pull back exactly when the local economy needs them. The German Mittelstand, the layer of mid-sized manufacturers everyone admires, is what that kind of banking produces. The United States had something like it. In 1984 it had 14,496 banks. It has 4,238 now.

So the first piece is bank density: make it possible to start a bank again. Only 86 new banks have been chartered since 2010, and the FDIC, hardly a radical body, has said publicly that it needs to find ways to encourage more. The second piece is credit guidance, the deliberate steering of bank lending toward production rather than asset purchase. Werner's history of Japan's postwar window guidance is the standard reference, and the honest version notes that the same tool, misused in the 1980s, helped inflate Japan's bubble. The third is structural separation: banks that take deposits and lend to businesses on one side, institutions that speculate on the other. The Warren–McCain bill to restore a version of Glass–Steagall is one draft of it.

The chart is the clock. Net federal interest is about a trillion dollars this year, more than defense, more than Medicaid. The Congressional Budget Office projects it at $2.1 trillion by 2036. That's the cost of the walkway from chapter 4, and it rises whether or not anyone decides anything.

The objections are serious and the book takes them seriously. Credit guidance is an invitation to cronyism; whoever decides what counts as "productive" will be lobbied. Small banks failed by the thousand in the 1930s and again in the savings-and-loan collapse of the 1980s. Glass–Steagall wouldn't have touched Lehman or AIG, the institutions that actually failed in 2008. All granted. None of it is an argument for the status quo, in which a few dozen institutions lend into buildings and the interest bill compounds. It's an argument for doing the reform carefully. The alternative is to keep treating the symptoms, and the previous eight chapters are what that looks like.

Appendix

How to read this tape

Every number rests on a choice. These are the choices.

The unit

Hours or years of a construction worker's pay (BLS average hourly earnings, production and nonsupervisory, construction; 2,080 hours a year). It needs no price index, and "one construction income supported a family" is where the book starts.

When a deflator is unavoidable

Real-wage charts use CPI-U and say so. BLS's own research series (CPI-U-RS) would show slightly higher real growth. Nothing on this tape is deflated with ShadowStats or Chapwood.

Housing

Median new-home sales price (Census/HUD) since 1963. Decennial Census owner-reported values before that, reported separately and never spliced. Mortgage math: 20% down, 30-year fixed, principal and interest only. Tax and insurance are a to-do.

Contested statistics

Dissident measures are included and described, with their weaknesses in the same paragraph. ShadowStats is credited for direction and excluded for arithmetic. Every lawsuit is "alleges" until ruled. Villains are incentives, not people.

Data snapshot

All FRED series pulled September 30, 2026. Latest observations: CPI-U Aug 2026 = 334.98; construction pay Aug 2026 = $39.36/hr; median new home Q2 2026 = $410,700; 30-year mortgage Sept 24, 2026 = 7.03%.

Known weak spots

The 1913–1919 wage values need verification. No home-price data exists before 1940. KFF shows no post-2020 denial surge. NAR's first-time-buyer age is an outlier. The construction-wage decline has documented non-monetary causes.
